Idea 1 of 8

Bits and qubits

A qubit is the quantum version of a bit: when you read it you still get a 0 or a 1, but before you read it, it holds a direction rather than a value, and that direction sets the odds.

Reading a qubit, called measuring it, gives one bit and destroys the arrow: afterwards the qubit really is the 0 or 1 you saw. So "0 and 1 at the same time" is misleading; the qubit does not secretly hold both answers for you to look up. The angle around the equator, the phase, is invisible to a direct read, yet it is what lets qubit states cancel each other later.

In one line

A qubit is an arrow, not a hidden 0‑or‑1. Reading it gives you one bit, with odds set by where the arrow points.

Idea 2 of 8

Superposition: one number per possible answer

With several qubits, the machine keeps one number for every possible answer, and unlike probabilities, those numbers can be negative.

These numbers are called amplitudes, and holding all of them at once is what superposition means. The count doubles with every qubit: 3 qubits have 8, and 50 qubits have about a million billion. That is the "exponential" in every quantum pitch.

That alone is not a speedup: reading the qubits returns one answer, picked with those odds, and the rest are gone. A randomised program also has a probability per outcome; the one change is that amplitudes carry a sign.

In one line

Superposition is a list of signed numbers, one per possible answer, and you only ever get to read one answer.

Idea 3 of 8

Interference: how the right answer wins

Because amplitudes have signs, two routes to the same answer can add up or cancel out, and every quantum algorithm is a way of arranging that so wrong answers cancel and the right one is left.

Block one route and B starts catching photons again: removing a way to get there makes the outcome more likely. Amplitudes add before they are squared into odds.

A quantum algorithm runs the same trick across many qubits: it steers the amplitudes so paths to wrong answers cancel and paths to the right answer reinforce, then reads once.

In one line

Quantum computers don't try every answer at once; they make wrong answers cancel and the right one add up, then read once.

Idea 4 of 8

Entanglement: linked outcomes

Two qubits can share a single state, so that each one looks random on its own but their results always line up, and quantum algorithms need this to do anything a normal computer can't.

Two sealed envelopes with matching cards also always agree, so why is this special? Tilt both measurements to the same new angle and they still agree, and at different angles they agree in a pattern no pre-written answers can reproduce. John Bell proved that in 1964; the experiments that confirmed it won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics.

It cannot carry a message: B's results on their own stay a 50/50 coin whatever A does, and the match only shows when the two lists are compared. For computing, entanglement is necessary but not sufficient; a computation that creates little of it can be simulated efficiently on an ordinary computer.

In one line

Entangled qubits share one state: random one at a time, perfectly correlated together. A resource, not a wire.

Idea 5 of 8

What it actually speeds up

A quantum computer is not a faster CPU; it is a narrow accelerator that wins big only on problems with a hidden regularity that interference can pick out.

For factoring, the best known classical method slows down far faster than Shor's algorithm as numbers grow, so the gap widens without limit. For unstructured search, Grover's algorithm (searching a list with no order to exploit) needs only the square root of the checks, but each quantum step is so much slower than a CPU's that breaking even takes months on plausible assumptions.

The hardest scheduling and routing puzzles (the NP-complete ones) are not believed to fall at all, and no optimisation or machine-learning workload has a demonstrated quantum advantage today.

In one line

Big wins need hidden structure: code-breaking and chemistry yes, generic search barely, generic optimisation not shown.

Idea 6 of 8

Errors: why one good qubit costs hundreds of bad ones

Today's qubits slip about once every thousand operations, useful programs need about one slip in a trillion, and the classical fix of keeping copies is off the table, because a qubit cannot be copied.

One reliable qubit built this way is called a logical qubit; the hardware qubits it is made of are physical qubits. The trick only works below a threshold (roughly 1 slip per 100 operations for this design). Below it, a bigger patch means fewer failures. Above it, a bigger patch just adds more ways to fail.

So a machine's qubit count matters less than how often each qubit slips: a qubit ten times better cuts a useful machine roughly fourfold. Try it in the physical-per-logical calculator.

In one line

Error correction trades many unreliable qubits for one reliable one, about 900 to 1 for today's quality, and only works if the qubits are already good enough.

Idea 7 of 8

Where the hardware stands, November 2026

Error correction now demonstrably works in the lab, on machines roughly 50 to 10,000 times smaller than the jobs that would matter.

The real milestone is that error correction behaves as theory said. Google's Willow chip grew its patch from size 3 to 5 to 7 and failures fell at each step (Nature, Dec 2024). Quantinuum reports about 5 failures per million rounds of storage at size 7 on Helios, in a company report not yet peer reviewed (Quantinuum, Oct 2026).

Be careful with "N logical qubits" headlines. The biggest 2026 numbers mostly use codes that only detect slips and throw away the bad runs, which does not scale; this table shows what each announcement meant. Vendors promise small error-corrected machines around 2029 (IBM's Starling targets 200 logical qubits), and DARPA is formally testing whether anyone can reach useful scale by 2033.

In one line

The physics works; the machines are still far too small, and "logical qubit" in a headline often means less than it sounds.

Idea 8 of 8

What to do now

The one thing most engineers should act on today is replacing RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, because encrypted data recorded now can be decrypted whenever a big enough machine shows up.

In practice: inventory where your systems use RSA, Diffie–Hellman or elliptic-curve keys, and plan the move to the NIST post-quantum standards (dates per NIST IR 8547). Symmetric ciphers and hashes are only mildly affected; longer keys cover it.

Beyond crypto, the long-term prize is chemistry and materials, and the first real win will probably look modest: one molecule, checked against experiment. Treat pitches about quantum optimisation and machine learning with suspicion.

In one line

Start the post-quantum migration now; for everything else, watch the error rates, not the qubit counts.
