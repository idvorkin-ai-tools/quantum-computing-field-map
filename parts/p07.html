Dominant Institutions in Quantum Computing

Quantum Computing, Mapped · part 07 of 09 · current to November 2026
TL;DR — Who is actually building quantum computers, and how far along are they? Group the field by physical modality rather than by logo, because the modality fixes the engineering trade-offs. Superconducting (Google, IBM, USTC) and trapped ions (Quantinuum) have the strongest error-correction results. Neutral atoms (Harvard/MIT/QuEra, Atom Computing with Microsoft, Infleqtion) are scaling fastest in raw qubit count. Photonics and topological qubits are big bets that have not yet published results from a large working processor. The thing to remember: each claim comes with a review status, and a peer-reviewed memory experiment, a company technical report and a roadmap slide are three different kinds of evidence.

Pages 04–06 covered what a quantum computer is and why error correction decides whether it can ever do useful work. This page is the map of who is attempting it. It works like a vendor landscape you might write before choosing a database: group by architecture, note the best demonstrated result with its evidence, keep the promises in a separate column, and mark how well each figure has been checked. Page 08 then tells the 2024–2026 results in order. Here the question is structural. Which bets are on the table, who holds them, and how much has each one actually proven?

How to read this landscape

The useful axis is the modality, meaning the physical system that holds the qubit. A modality is roughly like a process node plus an ISA. It sets the gate speed, the native connectivity, the dominant error mechanisms and the scaling wall. Two companies in the same modality face the same physics. Two companies in different modalities often aren't solving the same problem.

Every number on this page carries one of these tags:

  • peer-reviewed published in a refereed journal (Nature, PRL). Referees checked the methods. That makes the result more trustworthy, but it can still be wrong.
  • preprint an arXiv paper or company technical report with real data, not yet refereed.
  • company claim a press release or roadmap. Treat a roadmap as a promise, not a result.
  • government an official programme document.

Throughout, keep page 06's distinction in mind. A physical qubit is a device. A logical qubit is an encoded qubit spread over many devices. The phrase "N logical qubits" covers everything from distance-2 error detection with post-selection up to distance-7 error correction with real-time decoding. The metrics that carry information are the code distance d, the suppression factor Λ (how much the logical error rate p_L drops each time d grows by 2), and whether the experiment corrected errors or only threw away the bad runs.

Superconducting circuits: Google, IBM, USTC and the rest

Superconducting transmons are microfabricated circuits cooled to millikelvin temperatures in a dilution refrigerator. Gates take tens of nanoseconds, so the clock is fast. Couplings are fixed and nearest-neighbour on a 2D chip. The surface code, which needs only a square grid, fits this hardware naturally. The scaling wall is engineering: wiring, cryogenic heat load, control electronics, and fabrication variation from one qubit to the next.

Google Quantum AI holds the strongest peer-reviewed surface-code result. On the 105-qubit Willow chip it ran a distance-7 surface-code memory with real-time decoding. The logical error fell by Λ ≈ 2.14 at each step d3→d5→d7, and the logical qubit lived about 2.4× longer than the best physical qubit on the chip (Nature, Dec 2024) peer-reviewed. "Below threshold" means that adding qubits makes the logical qubit better. That is the precondition for everything on page 06. In July 2026 Google reported a reinforcement-learning agent that tunes more than 1,000 control parameters during error correction. It cut logical error by about another 20% after expert calibration and set records of 7.72×10⁻⁴ logical error per cycle (distance-7 surface code) and 8.19×10⁻³ (distance-5 colour code) (Nature, Jul 2026) peer-reviewed. Separately, its "Quantum Echoes" out-of-time-order-correlator experiment claimed a verifiable advantage, about 13,000× faster than the best classical method on a supercomputer (Google, Oct 2025). The paper is peer-reviewed, but the 13,000× figure is Google's own estimate company claim.

IBM leads in published roadmaps and in machines that customers can use. Its newest processor, Nighthawk r3 (Nov 11 2026), has 520 programmable qubits (2,034 physical qubits on the chip in total) and is designed for 10,000 operations. IBM plans to link two of them into a 1,040-qubit system in 2027. The previous revision, Nighthawk r2, ran more than 7,500 operations on 120 qubits (IBM, Nov 2026) company claim. A Quantum System Two went live in Chicago on Nov 6 2026 (IBM) company claim. The fault-tolerance plan does not use the surface code. IBM is betting on qLDPC "bicycle" codes, which need far fewer physical qubits per logical qubit but also need couplers longer than nearest-neighbour. The roadmap is Starling (200 logical qubits, 100 million gates, by 2029, Poughkeepsie), then Blue Jay (~2,000 logical qubits, 1 billion operations, 2033+) (IBM, Jun 2025) company claim.

IBM's most instructive 2026 result came with the University of Chicago. The press release announced 70 logical qubits, 2,415 logical two-qubit operations and 468 logical T gates, about 15 minutes on hardware, with effective logical error ~10× below physical (IBM, Jul 2026) company claim. The paper preprint describes error detection with syndrome post-selection on a Heron r3 machine, and it gives a certified lower bound on fidelity. Independent researchers then simulated the original 70-qubit instance exactly with tensor networks, using about 159 H100-GPU-hours, and confirmed the fidelity estimate. Version 3 of the paper switched to a 64-qubit periodic-boundary circuit (76 physical qubits, 314 T gates, fidelity lower bound 0.349 at 95% confidence) to close that classical shortcut. Page 08 tells this story in full. The structural point here is that IBM published its circuits openly, which allowed them to be simulated, and that is how advantage claims ought to be tested.

USTC (University of Science and Technology of China) is the main public face of China's programme. Its 107-qubit Zuchongzhi 3.2 ran a distance-7 surface code with Λ = 1.40(6), the first below-threshold surface code outside the US (PRL, Dec 2025) peer-reviewed. Google's Λ is larger, and as exercise 1 shows, that gap compounds very quickly.

Alice & Bob (France) builds superconducting cat qubits. These encode a qubit in two coherent states of a microwave resonator, so bit-flip errors are exponentially suppressed in hardware and the code only has to correct phase flips. On paper that is a large reduction in overhead. Its October 2026 results under the French PROQCIMA programme still measured a logical error rate above the error-correction threshold (TQI, Oct 2026) company claim. Rigetti (US) and IQM (Finland) also build superconducting processors and sell them on-premises or through clouds. This page has no checked 2024–2026 figures for them, so they appear here by modality only.

Trapped ions: Quantinuum, IonQ and Oxford Ionics

Trapped ions are single charged atoms held in electromagnetic traps and driven by lasers or microwaves. All ions of one species are identical, so there is no fabrication spread. Coherence times are long, and two-qubit fidelities are the best of any modality. Ions can be shuttled around the trap, which gives all-to-all connectivity. The cost is speed: gates and transport are orders of magnitude slower than in transmons. The scaling wall is fitting more ions and more optical control into one system.

Quantinuum launched Helios on Nov 5 2025. It has 98 barium-ion qubits with all-to-all connectivity and 99.921% two-qubit gate fidelity. The company also reported 48 error-corrected logical qubits at close to 2:1 encoding (HPCwire, Nov 2025). The hardware paper is peer-reviewed (Nature 2026), but the 48-logical figure is the company's framing company claim. In October 2026 Quantinuum published a technical report on colour codes at d = 3, 5, 7 (Quantinuum report; TQI coverage) preprint. Logical memory error per syndrome cycle was 5.5×10⁻⁴, 3.8×10⁻⁵ and ~5×10⁻⁶. That gives Λ ≈ 12, and the "99.9995%" headline is this d = 7 per-cycle memory fidelity. The logical two-qubit Clifford gate error was ~7×10⁻⁵ at d = 5. No d = 7 gate was run, because the machine does not have enough qubits. The code uses up to 37 physical qubits per logical. The roadmap is Sol (2027), then Apollo, a universal fault-tolerant machine, in 2029 (HPCwire) company claim.

IonQ acquired Oxford Ionics in 2025 for about $1.075B, combining a laser-gate company with one that controls ions electronically on chip. At its September 2026 investor day it described "Superion": ~10,000 physical qubits, a laboratory fault-tolerance demonstration targeted for 2027, and ~2M physical qubits by 2030. It disclosed no Superion fidelity or logical-qubit data (TQI, Sep 2026) company claim. A roadmap with no published error rates gives you nothing to calibrate against. Keep it in the promises column until data appears.

Neutral atoms: Harvard/MIT/QuEra, Atom Computing + Microsoft, Infleqtion

Neutral-atom machines hold hundreds of uncharged atoms in optical tweezers and entangle them by exciting them briefly into Rydberg states. Their strengths are scale and reconfigurability: atoms can be physically moved mid-circuit, so the connectivity graph is software. That suits qLDPC and transversal-gate schemes that a fixed chip cannot run. The weaknesses are slow cycles, atom loss (an atom simply disappears and has to be replaced), and two-qubit fidelities that still trail ions.

Harvard, MIT and QuEra published a 448-atom reconfigurable architecture showing the components of fault tolerance below threshold: logical entanglement, logical magic states, teleportation, and dozens of layers of error correction (Nature, Nov 2025; Harvard Gazette) peer-reviewed. QuEra and AWS plan "Libra", a fault-tolerant system with 256 logical qubits on Amazon Braket, targeted for 2028 (PostQuantum) company claim.

Atom Computing and Microsoft showed 24 entangled logical qubits in a GHZ state, and 28 logical qubits from 112 atoms running Bernstein–Vazirani (the algorithm from page 04), with ~10% logical error against ~42% physical (Microsoft, Nov 2024) company claim. A 10% logical error rate is nowhere near useful. The meaningful part is that the encoding beat the bare qubits. Their next machine is "Magne", a 50-logical-qubit system with 1,225 atoms for Denmark's QuNorth initiative, announced in July 2025 as ready for use by the end of 2026 (QCR, Jul 2025) company claim.

Infleqtion announced 30 entangled logical qubits on its Sqale machine. These were 80 atoms arranged as 10 blocks of the [[8,3,2]] code. Distance 2 means the code can detect one error but cannot correct it, so the results were post-selected, with lost atoms repaired (Infleqtion, Sep 2026) company claim. Its roadmap targets 100 logical qubits by 2028 and 1,000 by 2030 (same source) company claim. Pasqal (France) is the other major neutral-atom company. This page has no checked figures for it.

The long bets: photonics, topological qubits, silicon spins

Photonics. Photonic qubits are particles of light. They barely decohere and do not need millikelvin cooling, and they can be made in existing silicon-photonics fabs. The catch is that photons do not interact with each other, so entangling gates are probabilistic. Photonic architectures therefore build large entangled resource states and compute by measuring them, and photon loss is the dominant error. PsiQuantum raised $1B in a September 2025 Series E at a $7B valuation. It is building utility-scale sites in Brisbane (now Moreton Bay) and Chicago (TQI, Sep 2025) company claim. The bet is that if fab-scale manufacturing works, qubit count stops being the bottleneck. Xanadu (Canada) pursues photonics with squeezed light and is also known for the PennyLane software library. No checked figures here.

Topological qubits. Microsoft's in-house approach tries to store quantum information in Majorana zero modes, where it would be protected by topology rather than by active correction. If it works, the error rate falls at the hardware level. Its Majorana 1 claim (Feb 2025) remains contested. A critique by Henry Legg was published in Nature in June 2026. Microsoft replied and stands by the work, and critics say the Majorana 2 announcement (June 2026) has the same problems (Hackaday, Jun 2026). This is the one modality where the basic question is still open: does the qubit exist at all? Microsoft also hedges by partnering with Atom Computing on neutral atoms (above).

Silicon spin qubits store a qubit in the spin of a single electron in a silicon device that looks much like a transistor. The appeal is CMOS manufacturing and tiny qubits. The difficulty is device variability and wiring in very dense arrays. Diraq (Sydney) has entered DARPA's QBI final stage (see below). Intel also develops silicon spin qubits. This page has no checked performance figures for either.

Clouds and governments: access, money, referees

Most engineers will never stand next to a dilution refrigerator. They will submit circuits through an API. There are two cloud patterns:

  • Vendor clouds. IBM runs its own fleet and the Qiskit stack. Google, so far, mostly publishes research results rather than selling machine time.
  • Hyperscaler marketplaces. Amazon Braket and Azure Quantum resell time on third-party machines from several modalities behind one SDK and one bill. They also run high-performance simulators, which is where nearly all real development happens today.

The hyperscalers are also hedging. Amazon is the planned host for QuEra's Libra. Microsoft runs both a topological programme and the Atom Computing partnership. Read that as a cloud provider making sure it controls access to whichever modality wins. It does not tell you which one it expects to win.

Governments do three different jobs in this field, and you should keep them separate.

Referee. The most useful programme for an outsider is DARPA's Quantum Benchmarking Initiative (QBI). It asks whether any approach can reach utility scale, defined as computational value exceeding cost, by 2033. On Oct 7 2026 Atom Computing (neutral atoms), Diraq (silicon spins), IBM (superconducting) and IonQ (trapped ions) entered Stage C, the final stage. Microsoft and PsiQuantum were already in Stage C through the US2QC pilot (DARPA, Oct 2026) government. DARPA says explicitly that QBI is not a competition and is not meant to pick winners. Stage C means a government team will check whether the proposed machines can be built and run as designed. It is the closest thing the field has to an independent design review of the roadmaps in the table below. Note that four modalities are in Stage C. Even the referee is not ruling any of them out.

Funder. The US coordinates federal research under the National Quantum Initiative. China's effort is most visible through USTC's published results. In Europe, national programmes such as France's PROQCIMA (the programme behind the Alice & Bob results above) and Denmark's QuNorth (the buyer of Magne) sit alongside the EU-level Quantum Flagship. Public money shapes which modalities survive the long gap before any revenue arrives.

Deadline-setter. For a software engineer the most consequential government action is not about hardware at all. NIST's IR 8547, finalised Oct 27 2026, deprecates all quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms (RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, DH, ECDH) after 2030 and disallows them after 2035, regardless of key size (NIST IR 8547) government. Those dates do not depend on which company in the table wins. Page 09 turns them into a migration checklist.

Check yourself

  1. Λ compounds code — Assume Λ stays constant as distance grows (a strong assumption). Starting from Google's 7.72×10⁻⁴ per cycle at d=7, how many distance steps of +2 does it take to reach 10⁻¹² per cycle with Λ = 2.14? With USTC's Λ = 1.40 from the same starting point? With Quantinuum's Λ ≈ 12 starting from 5×10⁻⁶? Compute steps = ln(p_L/10⁻¹²)/ln Λ in numpy, then d = 7 + 2·⌈steps⌉. A good answer gets about d≈61, d≈129 and d≈21, and notes that patch size grows as d², so a small difference in Λ becomes an order of magnitude in qubits. It also says why the constant-Λ assumption is doubtful: correlated errors and leakage start to matter at large d.
  2. Starling against the plain surface code code — IBM promises 200 logical qubits running 100 million gates. Treat that as 10⁸ rounds and spend a total failure budget of 0.1, so 200 · 10⁸ · p_L ≤ 0.1. With the site heuristic p_L ≈ 0.1·(p/p_th)^((d+1)/2), p = 10⁻³ and p_th = 1%, find the smallest odd d and the physical-qubit count at 2d² − 1 per patch. (Expect d = 21, 881 per logical, ~176k qubits. Check it against the Starling preset in the page 06 calculator.) Then explain in two sentences why IBM's bet on bicycle codes is about shrinking that 881, and what hardware feature it needs in return.
  3. Grade the press release — Pick two rows from the table that report a logical-qubit count. For each one, write down the distance, whether errors were detected or corrected, whether results were post-selected, whether the figure measures memory or gates, and the review status. A good answer explains why "30 logical qubits" at distance 2 and "one logical qubit" at distance 7 are not comparable, and says which one is closer to a fault-tolerant computer.
