Recent Breakthroughs

Quantum Computing, Mapped · part 08 of 09 · current to November 2026
TL;DR — What actually changed between late 2024 and November 2026? The big one: error correction now works the way theory said it would. Several groups grew a code's distance and watched the logical error rate fall, which is the signal that the overhead stays bounded. At the same time "logical qubit" turned into a marketing unit. It now covers everything from distance-7 correction to distance-2 error detection with post-selection, so the headline number alone tells you little. Carry one habit away from this page: before you count logical qubits, ask what code they use, at what distance, and whether errors are corrected or only detected.

Pages 04–06 gave you the model: amplitudes, unitaries, and a surface code whose logical error falls as (p/p_th)^((d+1)/2) once p < p_th. Page 07 covered who is building machines. This page checks the model against the record from the last two years. It runs in date order, gives each claim its source and review status, and spends the most space on the one episode that best shows how a quantum-advantage claim gets tested in public. Every 2024–2026 fact here links to its source. A peer-reviewed tag means a journal refereed it. preprint means it has not been refereed yet. company claim means a press release or a company's own technical report.

Late 2024 to 2025: error correction starts to work

Microsoft + Atom Computing, November 2024. On a neutral-atom machine they reported 24 entangled logical qubits in a GHZ state, and 28 logical qubits made from 112 physical atoms running Bernstein–Vazirani. Logical error was about 10%, against about 42% for the same circuit on bare physical qubits. (Azure blog) company claim That works out to four physical atoms per logical qubit. A distance-7 surface-code patch needs 97 (2d² − 1), so you already see the vocabulary problem this page keeps coming back to.

Willow, December 2024. Google Quantum AI ran a surface-code memory at distances 3, 5 and 7 on its 105-qubit superconducting chip Willow. Each step up in distance divided the logical error rate by Λ ≈ 2.14. The logical qubit lived about 2.4× longer than the best physical qubit on the chip, and the experiment included real-time decoding. (Nature) peer-reviewed

Recall what Λ means. In the site-wide heuristic, raising d by 2 multiplies p_L by p/p_th, so Λ = p_L(d) / p_L(d+2) ≈ p_th/p. Any Λ > 1 means you are below threshold, and extra qubits now buy exponentially better logical qubits. Above threshold, Λ ≲ 1 and a bigger code makes things worse. A value of 2.14 is modest: each extra two units of distance only halves the error, while the patch grows from 97 to 161 qubits going from d = 7 to d = 9. But it is on the right side of 1, measured across three distances, and that is the question that matters.

Majorana 1, February 2025. Microsoft announced a topological-qubit chip. The claim is still contested. A critique by Henry Legg was published in Nature in June 2026. Microsoft replied and stands by the work, and critics say the Majorana 2 chip announced on 2 June 2026 has the same problems. (Hackaday summary) contested For an engineer the practical takeaway: until the dispute settles, leave topological qubits out of any timeline you plan around.

RSA gets cheaper on paper, May 2025. Craig Gidney estimated that 2048-bit RSA could be factored with fewer than a million noisy qubits in just under a week. The assumptions are 0.1% gate error, a 1 µs surface-code cycle and a nearest-neighbour grid. That is 20× fewer qubits than the 2019 estimate of 20 million. (arXiv 2505.15917) preprint The hardware did not change. The algorithms and the error-correction engineering did, and they moved faster than the hardware.

Roadmaps, June–July 2025. IBM published its fault-tolerant plan: Starling, with 200 logical qubits and 100 million gates by 2029 using qLDPC "bicycle" codes, then Blue Jay at about 2,000 logical qubits from 2033 on. (IBM) company claim Atom Computing and Microsoft announced "Magne", a 50-logical-qubit machine of 1,225 atoms for Denmark's QuNorth, announced in July 2025 as ready for use by the end of 2026. (QCR) company claim These are promises. They are listed here only so you can check them against what gets delivered.

Quantum Echoes, October 2025. Google measured an out-of-time-order correlator (OTOC) on Willow. The quantity is sensitive to how information spreads through a many-body system, and it can be checked: you can run it again on another quantum machine, or compare it with a physical experiment. Google called it the first verifiable quantum advantage and estimated it ran about 13,000× faster than the best classical method on a supercomputer. A proof of principle with UC Berkeley compared the method against NMR data on 15- and 28-atom molecules. (Google) peer-reviewed, though the 13,000× figure is Google's own estimate. "Verifiable" matters because the older random-circuit-sampling advantage results produced output nobody could check.

November 2025, two neutral-atom and ion milestones. Harvard, MIT and QuEra built a reconfigurable architecture of 448 atoms. On it they showed the parts of fault tolerance working below threshold: logical entanglement, logical magic states, teleportation, and dozens of rounds of error correction. (Nature, Harvard Gazette) peer-reviewed The point is breadth. This was a full set of fault-tolerant operations, not one memory experiment. Quantinuum launched Helios the same month: 98 barium-ion qubits, all-to-all connectivity, 99.921% two-qubit gate fidelity. The hardware paper is peer-reviewed. The company also reported 48 error-corrected logical qubits at "close to 2:1" encoding, and that figure is a company claim. Its roadmap names Sol for 2027 and Apollo, a universal fault-tolerant machine, for 2029. (HPCwire) company claim

Zuchongzhi 3.2, December 2025. USTC ran a distance-7 surface code using 97 qubits of a 107-qubit superconducting chip and measured Λ = 1.40(6). It is the first below-threshold surface code outside the US. Google's 2.14 is still the stronger result. (Physical Review Letters) peer-reviewed Note that 97 = 2·7²−1, exactly the patch size from page 06.

2026: tuning, scaling claims, and an advantage claim tested in public

Pinnacle, February 2026. Iceberg Quantum estimated RSA-2048 at under 100,000 physical qubits using generalized-bicycle qLDPC codes, with the same 10⁻³ error and 1 µs cycle assumptions as Gidney. The catch is the one page 06 flagged: it needs non-local connectivity and fast qLDPC decoding. (arXiv 2602.11457) preprint

Learning the calibration, July 2026. Google let a reinforcement-learning agent tune more than 1,000 control parameters on Willow while error correction was running. It gave about 20% lower logical error on top of expert calibration. Records: 7.72×10⁻⁴ logical error per cycle on a distance-7 surface code and 8.19×10⁻³ on a distance-5 colour code. (Nature, blog) peer-reviewed This is unglamorous but it matters. A million-qubit machine cannot be calibrated by hand, so calibration has to become software.

IBM + University of Chicago, July 2026. This one gets its own section below.

Infleqtion, September 2026. The company reported 30 entangled logical qubits on its Sqale neutral-atom machine, from 80 atoms arranged as 10 blocks of an [[8,3,2]] code, with post-selection and atom-loss repair. Distance 2 means any single error is detected and none is corrected. A full paper has been promised but is not out. (Infleqtion, analysis) company claim The stated roadmap is 100 logical qubits by 2028 and 1,000 by 2030. company claim

DARPA QBI Stage C, 7 October 2026. DARPA's Quantum Benchmarking Initiative asks one question: can any approach reach utility scale, meaning value greater than cost, by 2033? Atom Computing, Diraq, IBM and IonQ moved into Stage C, the final stage. Microsoft and PsiQuantum were already there through the earlier US2QC pilot. (DARPA) government This is the closest thing the field has to an independent referee of roadmaps.

Quantinuum "99.9995%", 19 October 2026. On Helios, Quantinuum ran triangular colour codes at d = 3, 5, 7. Logical memory error per syndrome-extraction cycle came out at 5.5×10⁻⁴, 3.8×10⁻⁵ and about 5×10⁻⁶, a suppression of Λ ≈ 12. Be exact about the headline: 99.9995% is the per-cycle memory fidelity at d = 7. The logical two-qubit gate was measured at d = 5, with error about 7×10⁻⁵. No d = 7 gate was run because the machine did not have enough qubits. The d = 7 colour code uses up to 37 physical qubits per logical. The result is about 10× better than the company's September 2026 "Helix" code result, which used 10 physical per logical. (technical report, Quantum Insider) preprint company claim, not peer reviewed yet. A Λ of 12 against Willow's 2.14 is a big gap, but the two do not compare one-for-one. Trapped-ion error-correction cycles are orders of magnitude slower than superconducting ones, so "per cycle" is not "per second".

Alice & Bob, October 2026. Results from phase 1 of the French PROQCIMA programme show steady progress with cat qubits. The measured logical error rate is still above threshold. (Quantum Insider) company claim It is worth listing because it is a negative result reported straight.

IBM hardware, November 2026. IBM opened a Quantum System Two in Chicago on 6 November (IBM) company claim. On 11 November it announced Nighthawk r3: 520 programmable qubits out of 2,034 physical qubits on the chip, designed for 10,000 operations, with a plan to link two chips into a 1,040-qubit system in 2027. Nighthawk r2 in September had passed 7,500 operations on 120 qubits. (IBM) company claim These are physical qubits running uncorrected circuits. Treat them as a step toward Starling, not as logical qubits.

The IBM–UChicago episode: how an advantage claim gets tested

On 30 July 2026, IBM and the University of Chicago announced quantum advantage "on logical circuits". The press release listed 70 logical qubits, 2,415 logical two-qubit operations, 468 logical T gates and about 15 minutes on hardware. It reported an effective logical error about 10× below physical, with the circuits posted to the open Quantum Advantage Tracker. (IBM press release) company claim

The paper, "Sampling hard circuits with verifiably high fidelity", says something narrower, and that something is still interesting. (arXiv 2607.25941) preprint It ran on IBM Boston, a Heron r3 processor, and used spacetime codes for error detection with post-selection. Syndrome checks flag runs that suffered an error, and those runs are thrown away. The surviving samples come with a certified lower bound on fidelity. That is a real contribution: most sampling-advantage experiments could not say how good their output was. But nothing was corrected. The "logical qubits" are encoded qubits whose errors get caught after the fact.

Then the community did its job. After the first version went up, independent researchers simulated the original 70-qubit open-boundary instance exactly with tensor networks. It took about 256 H100 GPUs for 37 minutes, roughly 159 GPU-hours. The simulation confirmed IBM's fidelity estimate, and it also showed that this instance was classically reachable. The open boundary gave the tensor network a shortcut through the circuit. Version 3 of the preprint (2 September 2026) changed the circuit to remove that shortcut: 64 logical qubits with periodic boundaries, on 76 physical qubits, with 314 T gates and a fidelity lower bound of 0.349 at 95% confidence. (arXiv, v3) preprint

Three lessons, none of them about IBM in particular:

  • "Advantage" is a claim about the best classical algorithm, so it is falsifiable and has a shelf life. Publishing the circuits openly is what let the claim be tested within weeks. That is the system working, and it should count in the authors' favour.
  • Simulation cost depends on structure, not just qubit count. 70 qubits sounds like 2⁷⁰ amplitudes, but tensor networks pay for entanglement across cuts of the circuit, not for raw width. A boundary condition was enough to change the verdict.
  • Post-selected detection does not scale as it stands. If each run survives with probability a, the cost per kept sample is 1/a, and a falls exponentially with circuit size. It is a good tool for certifying medium-sized experiments. It is not a road to running Shor.

Read the table by columns. Physical qubits per logical qubit runs from about 1.2 to 97, a factor of 80. That ratio tells you which regime you are looking at. Codes with low ratios either detect only, which is cheap but needs post-selection, or need high-rate code families whose good distance only shows up at much larger block sizes. Codes with high ratios correct errors and show exponential suppression with distance. Page 06 showed that a useful machine needs d in the high teens to high twenties for the plain surface code. qLDPC and colour codes lower the ratio, but no row in this table pairs a ratio near 2:1 with a distance that corrects errors. So when a headline gives you the logical-qubit count, divide physical by logical yourself before you believe anything.

Carry this: a logical qubit is not a unit. It is a code plus a distance plus a decision about what happens when an error is found. "Below threshold with Λ > 1 as distance grows" is the result that matters for fault tolerance, and as of November 2026 Google, USTC, Harvard/QuEra and Quantinuum have shown it in different forms. A larger count of distance-2 logical qubits with post-selection is a different and much weaker kind of result.

Cryptography: the deadlines got firm

The hardware is still years from breaking RSA. The policy clock has stopped waiting for it. On 27 October 2026, NIST finalised IR 8547. Every quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithm (RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, DH, MQV, ECDH) is deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035, regardless of key size. The 2024 draft had let parameters at 128-bit security or above skip deprecation, and the final version drops that exemption. The report also cites US federal requirements for the most sensitive systems to use post-quantum key establishment by 31 December 2030 and post-quantum signatures by 31 December 2031. (NIST IR 8547) government The replacements are already standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), 204 (ML-DSA) and 205 (SLH-DSA), all final since August 2024. FN-DSA (Falcon) and the backup KEM HQC are still in progress. (NIST PQC) government

Set the dates next to the resource estimates: 20M qubits for RSA-2048 (2019), under 1M (Gidney, 2025), under 100k with qLDPC (Pinnacle, 2026, preprint). The machine is not here, but the target keeps getting closer. And "harvest now, decrypt later" means ciphertext recorded today is exposed whenever the machine does arrive. Any secret that has to stay secret past the mid-2030s is already in scope. Page 09 turns this into a to-do list.

How to read the next press release

Six questions. Most announcements answer three of them, and the missing three are usually the story.

  1. What distance? Distance 2 detects one error and corrects none. Distance 3 corrects one. Fault-tolerant algorithms need distances in the teens or higher (page 06). No distance given means assume it is small.
  2. Detection or correction? If errors only flag the run for discarding, the result is post-selected, and the cost grows exponentially with circuit size.
  3. What is the post-selection rate? If runs are discarded, what fraction survives, and how does that fraction scale as the circuit grows?
  4. Memory or gates? Holding a logical state (memory) is easier than computing on it. Check which operations were done at the headline distance. Quantinuum's best number is memory at d=7, while its gate number is at d=5.
  5. Peer reviewed? Journal paper, preprint, company technical report, or a press release with a paper "to follow"? The tags on this page exist to answer this one.
  6. Has anyone tried to simulate it classically? For any advantage claim, were the circuits published, and has a tensor-network group taken a shot at them? The IBM–UChicago case shows this can happen in weeks, and the claim either survives or gets refined.

Add one more question for roadmaps: is there data from the hardware generation the roadmap builds on? A target year with no fidelity numbers behind it (several are listed on page 07) is a fundraising document, not a forecast.

Check yourself

  1. Extract Λ code — In numpy, take Quantinuum's per-cycle memory errors [5.5e-4, 3.8e-5, 5e-6] at d = 3, 5, 7 and compute both step ratios and their geometric mean. Do the same from Willow's Λ = 2.14. A good answer notes that the two step ratios differ (about 14 and 8), so "Λ ≈ 12" is a fit over few points, and that a large Λ from only three distances has wide error bars.
  2. Extrapolate to useful code — Assume Λ stays constant. Starting from Google's 7.72×10⁻⁴ at d = 7 (Λ = 2.14), find the smallest odd d with p_L ≤ 10⁻¹² per cycle, and the patch size 2d² − 1. Repeat from Quantinuum's 5×10⁻⁶ at d = 7 with Λ = 12. You should get about d = 61 (~7,400 physical) against about d = 21 (~880 physical). Then explain why this does not mean ions win: cycle time, connectivity limits on scaling, and whether Λ holds at larger d.
  3. The post-selection wall — A detection-only scheme discards a block-round with probability 2%. What fraction of runs survive with 10 blocks for 1 round? With 100 blocks for 100 rounds? How many runs does it take to keep 1,000 samples in each case? A good answer gets 0.98¹⁰ ≈ 0.82 against 0.98¹⁰⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁸⁸, and concludes that detection certifies experiments but cannot run long algorithms.
  4. Triage a headline — Take the "48 error-corrected logical qubits at close to 2:1" claim and run it through the six questions above. List which answers the public material gives and which it doesn't. Then say what one extra number would most change your view, and why. (Hint: the distance and whether that encoding corrects anything at all.)
