Conclusion

Quantum Computing, Mapped · part 09 of 09 · current to November 2026
TL;DR — What should a strong engineer believe about quantum computing in November 2026, and what should they do about it? Error correction now works in the lab: logical error rates fall as codes grow. Nobody has yet run a useful computation that a classical machine couldn't also do. Vendors promise fault-tolerant machines around 2029, and DARPA is formally asking whether utility scale is reachable by 2033. The one action that can't wait is post-quantum cryptography. NIST deprecates RSA and elliptic-curve public-key crypto after 2030 and disallows it after 2035, and data recorded today can be decrypted later.

Pages 01–08 built the argument piece by piece: what classical computation is, why amplitudes and interference give a different model, what that model can and cannot speed up, why noise makes it so expensive, who is building it, and what the last two years actually showed. This page turns that into positions you can hold, signals that should move them, and things to do on an ordinary Monday. Every current-state fact links to its source. Where I give a judgment rather than a fact, I say so.

What to believe, November 2026

Below-threshold error correction is real. In December 2024 Google's Willow chip ran a distance-7 surface-code memory. Its logical error rate fell by a factor Λ ≈ 2.14 each time the code distance went up by two, and the logical qubit outlived the best physical qubit by about 2.4×, with decoding in real time (Nature) peer-reviewed. That is the threshold theorem from page 06 seen working in hardware, not just on paper. USTC's Zuchongzhi 3.2 later reproduced a below-threshold distance-7 surface code at Λ = 1.40 (Physical Review Letters) peer-reviewed. Quantinuum's colour-code report on Helios gives Λ ≈ 12 and a per-cycle memory error of about 5×10⁻⁶ at d = 7 (technical report) preprint. Two caveats apply: that figure is memory, not gates, and the report has not been peer reviewed.

"N logical qubits" headlines mostly still mean error detection. The largest logical-qubit counts of 2026 came from codes that detect errors and throw away the bad runs. IBM and the University of Chicago's 70-logical-qubit result used post-selected error detection. Its first instance was then simulated exactly on GPUs, and the authors revised it to a harder 64-qubit circuit (arXiv 2607.25941) preprint. Infleqtion's 30 logical qubits use a distance-2 code that detects one error and corrects none (announcement) company claim. These are useful engineering steps, but they are not fault-tolerant computers.

Nobody has shown useful quantum advantage. Google's "Quantum Echoes" claims the first verifiable beyond-classical computation. The roughly 13,000× speedup in that claim is Google's own estimate (Google) peer-reviewed. It is a physics measurement, not a business workload. No optimisation or machine-learning result has shown quantum advantage. The honest scorecard from pages 02 and 05 still holds: the strong cases are simulating strongly correlated chemistry and materials, and Shor's algorithm, which breaks public-key crypto. Everything else is modest or unproven.

The topological route is still contested. Microsoft's Majorana claims drew a critique published in Nature in June 2026. Microsoft stands by its work, and critics say the later Majorana 2 has the same problems (Hackaday summary). Until a topological qubit is shown unambiguously, don't plan around it.

Read the DARPA row carefully: it is a question being asked, not a forecast. In October 2026 Atom Computing, Diraq, IBM and IonQ entered the programme's final Stage C, joining Microsoft and PsiQuantum, which were already there. That is six serious teams with very different hardware being independently tested against one yardstick. Treat that as the most credible scoreboard available.

This page's judgment (an opinion, not a sourced fact):

  • Small fault-tolerant machines (tens to a few hundred logical qubits running corrected gates, including the T gates) could plausibly appear between 2028 and the early 2030s. Every vendor date in the table assumes nothing slips. Historically, roadmaps in this field slip more often than they arrive early.
  • The first useful advantage will most likely come in chemistry or materials simulation, and will look modest: one molecule, one catalyst, verified against experiment. It won't look like a general-purpose speedup.
  • A cryptographically relevant machine needs a lot more than Starling-class hardware. By the site's plain surface-code model, RSA-2048 needs on the order of 1,400 logical qubits running for days (try the RSA preset in the page 06 calculator). The best published estimates are under a million noisy physical qubits for about a week (Gidney 2025) preprint. One preprint gets under 100,000 if long-range qLDPC connectivity can be built (Pinnacle) preprint. The date is uncertain, but those estimates keep falling, and that is the wrong direction to bet against.

What would change your mind

Don't track headlines. Track a handful of criteria, each of which separates real progress from good marketing. These are tests to apply, not predictions.

  • Corrected logical gates at distance ≥ 5, not just memory. A memory experiment shows a qubit can be stored. Computing needs two-qubit logical gates and non-Clifford operations (magic states) that also improve as the code grows.
  • Λ holding as d keeps growing. The surface-code heuristic p_L ≈ 0.1 · (p/p_th)^((d+1)/2) assumes errors stay independent. Correlated errors, leakage or cosmic-ray bursts would show up as Λ flattening at larger d. A clean d = 9 or d = 11 with steady Λ would be strong evidence.
  • Real-time decoding at scale. Each syndrome round has to be decoded in about a microsecond (for superconducting qubits) without a backlog. Watch for decoders running live on hundreds of logical qubits, not offline on recorded data.
  • A qLDPC code running in hardware. IBM's 2029 plan and the sub-100k RSA estimate both rest on codes that need non-local connections. A working bicycle-code memory on real hardware would cut the expected overhead by roughly an order of magnitude.
  • An advantage claim that survives a year of classical attack. The IBM–UChicago episode shows how this works: the claim is published, other groups try to simulate it classically, and the claim is revised. A result that is still standing after that, on a problem someone cares about, is the real milestone.
  • An unambiguous topological qubit. If it arrived it would change the overhead picture completely. If it doesn't, nothing in the plan above depends on it.

Signals that should not move you: physical qubit counts on their own, "logical qubits" with no stated distance, fidelities with no denominator, and any advantage claim about optimisation or ML that skips the best classical baseline.

What to do on Monday: post-quantum migration

You don't need a quantum computer to be affected by one. The threat model is "harvest now, decrypt later": an adversary records your TLS sessions or encrypted archives today and decrypts them once a large enough machine exists. Mosca's rule of thumb says when to worry. Let x be how long your data must stay secret, y how long your migration will take, and z how long until a cryptographically relevant QC exists.

if x + y > z then you are already late e.g. x = 10 yr (health records), y = 5 yr → exposed unless z > 15 yr, i.e. after ~2041

You don't control z, but the standards bodies have set your y for you. NIST finalised the replacements in August 2024: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, key encapsulation), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, signatures) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, hash-based signatures). FN-DSA (Falcon) and the backup KEM HQC are still being standardised (NIST PQC project). NIST IR 8547, finalised in October 2026, deprecates all quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms after 2030 and disallows them after 2035, regardless of key size. That covers RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, DH, MQV and ECDH. The report also cites US federal requirements for the most sensitive systems: PQC key establishment by December 31, 2030 and PQC signatures by December 31, 2031 (NIST IR 8547). Symmetric crypto and hashes are a different story. Grover gives only a quadratic speedup, and page 05 showed that error-correction overhead swamps it, so AES-256 and SHA-256/384 are fine.

The practical checklist:

  1. Inventory. Find every place you use public-key crypto: TLS termination, SSH, code signing, JWT/JWS signing keys, VPNs, HSM-backed keys, client libraries, and protocols you embed in firmware. Most organisations discover their real exposure in third-party dependencies.
  2. Rank by secrecy lifetime. Anything that must stay confidential past roughly 2035 is already exposed to harvest-now attacks. Key exchange protecting long-lived secrets comes first. Signatures matter later, because a forged signature only helps an attacker once the machine exists.
  3. Build crypto-agility. Algorithm choice should be configuration, not code. Negotiated suites, versioned key formats, and no hard-coded key or signature sizes. ML-DSA signatures and ML-KEM ciphertexts are far larger than ECDSA and ECDH ones, so check your buffer limits, certificate chains and MTU assumptions now.
  4. Prefer hybrid key exchange during the transition. Combine a classical exchange with ML-KEM so the session stays secure if either one holds. That hedges against an implementation flaw in the newer scheme while still defeating harvest-now attacks.
  5. Put the dates in your roadmap. 2030 deprecation and 2035 disallowance are planning facts, whatever you believe about quantum hardware timelines.
The thing to carry away: the quantum-hardware question is genuinely uncertain, but your crypto decision isn't. Error correction works in the lab, but nobody can yet build a useful machine, and every plausible date for a code-breaking one falls inside the lifetime of data you're encrypting today. Migrate to ML-KEM and ML-DSA on NIST's 2030/2035 schedule, make your systems crypto-agile, and judge every quantum headline by its code distance, whether it corrects or only detects errors, and whether it survives classical attack.

Learning hands-on, and what not to buy

Learn by simulating. The math on page 04 fits in a few dozen lines of numpy. The state is a length-2ⁿ complex vector. A gate on qubit k is a reshape, a tensor contraction and a reshape back. Measurement samples x with probability |a_x|². If you build that yourself, superposition, interference and entanglement turn into things you've debugged rather than metaphors. Around 25 qubits, the 2ⁿ blow-up stops being abstract: 2²⁵ complex128 amplitudes take 512 MiB, and each extra qubit doubles that.

Then move to a real framework. IBM's Qiskit documentation and Google's Cirq both let you build circuits, run them on simulators with realistic noise models, and submit small jobs to real hardware. Run Bernstein–Vazirani, a Bell-state test and a three-qubit repetition code on a noisy simulator, then on hardware. The gap between the two teaches you page 06 better than any prose.

What not to buy. Be sceptical of any pitch that puts a quantum speedup on optimisation, machine learning, portfolio construction or "big data" today. The reasons are structural:

  • Loading the data is the bottleneck. Encoding N classical numbers into amplitudes generally costs at least O(N), and you can read out only one sample per run (the Holevo bound from page 04). Aaronson's "Read the fine print" lists the conditions HHL-style speedups need, and real problems rarely meet them.
  • Quadratic speedups lose to overhead. A Grover-type √N gain has to pay for error correction that makes every logical operation many orders of magnitude slower than a classical one. Babbush et al. show that, for realistic machines, quadratic speedups don't pay off for problems of practical size.
  • Heuristics have no proof. QAOA and annealing are heuristics without guaranteed speedups. Several proposed exponential ML speedups were later "dequantised" by classical algorithms. As of this writing, no optimisation or ML workload has shown quantum advantage.

The legitimate exception is exploratory spending: a small team building fluency and running chemistry-style pilots, budgeted as R&D with no production expectations.

Check yourself

  1. Mosca for your own systems — Pick three datasets you're responsible for and estimate each one's secrecy lifetime x and your migration time y. Using NIST's 2035 disallowance as a conservative stand-in for z, which datasets are already exposed to harvest-now attacks? A good answer ranks them and names the key-exchange path (TLS, SSH, VPN, storage envelope keys) that has to move first.
  2. A 40-line statevector simulatorcode — In numpy, represent n qubits as a complex vector of length 2ⁿ. Write apply_1q(state, U, k) (reshape to [2]*n, tensordot on axis k, move the axis back) and apply_cnot(state, c, t), plus a sampler using np.random.choice with p = |a|². Check that H then H on |0⟩ returns |0⟩, that a Bell state gives only 00 and 11, and that Bernstein–Vazirani on 5 qubits recovers a hidden string in one query. Then time it at n = 20, 24 and 26 and explain the curve.
  3. Triage a headline — A press release says "we demonstrated 100 logical qubits with 99.99% fidelity". Write the six questions you'd ask before believing it means progress toward fault tolerance. A good answer covers: code distance, detection versus correction, the post-selection discard rate, memory versus gates (and whether non-Clifford gates were included), the review status, and whether anyone tried a classical simulation.
  4. Starling versus RSA — Open the page 06 calculator and run the RSA-2048-class preset, then the Starling preset. Roughly how many Starling-class machines' worth of logical qubits does the plain surface-code RSA run need? Then explain why Gidney's under-1M estimate and the qLDPC-based Pinnacle estimate are both lower than the calculator's figure. What techniques does the simple model leave out?
